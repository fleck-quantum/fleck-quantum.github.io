Quantum generative adversarial networks

Computer-assisted drug design relies on a number of techniques, one of which includes classical GANs.1 2 3 4 5 There are a few prospective studies investigating quantum pipelines to enhance the robustness of classical GAN for drug design,6 however none of them include style-based approaches. As an alternative to qGANs, the project also plans to investigate quantum diffusion models.

Diffusion models are considered the state of the art for classical image generation and are inspired by non-equilibrium thermodynamics. The idea is to define a Markov chain of diffusion steps to gradually add random noise to the input data, and then train a network to learn the reverse process, the denoising, such that the final constructed data is created out of random noise by using the trained denoising network.

Since the first seminal paper7 introducing a quantum architecture for diffusion models, there have been other works refining the quantum architecture,8 9 10 11 including latent approach for image synthesis12 13 and efficient implementation on current hardware. To date, there has been no attempt to apply quantum diffusion models to drug design.

The qGAN style-based approach is original research that came out of Switzerland and was first proposed by consortium member Uni Basel14—a technique that was later combined with a latent approach.15 The latter demonstrated great promise in increasing robustness and reducing model complexity for drug discovery applications but has, so far, not been deployed for anti-cancer treatments (with or without a contrast agent as a chemical complex).

Fleck will be the first project to automate a quantum drug-discovery pipeline using style-based qGANs in order to search for brain cancer imaging improvements and treatments.

Computational chemistry for MRI

Current literature reveals rapid advances in safer, high-performance MRI contrast agents, ranging from macrocyclic Gd1 2 complexes and Mn-based agents, to targeted ligands and nanoparticles (primarily iron oxide nanoparticles made of magnetite). In general though, the field still suffers from poor standardization, inconsistent characterization and a persistent gap between promising laboratory results and actual clinical translation.

Machine learning (ML) models are now frequently being used to facilitate the design and discovery of novel molecules and reduce the experimental workload. These typically look to identify the main features affecting the performance of the contrast agents, but only a handful of studies have attempted to use ML models to predict relaxivity. Robust, validated frameworks necessitate quantum-informed descriptors, consistent simulation workflows and multiscale data integration. The latter requisites are rarely found in combination in the literature, with most investigations focusing on isolated layers of the problem without combining them into a unified workflow.

Switzerland has developed an internationally recognised research landscape in MRI contrast agent development, with one of the main contributions coming from EPFL, which established the core principles of relaxivity, water exchange kinetics and electron-spin relaxation. ETH Zürich and Empa (Swiss Federal Laboratories for Materials Science and Technology) have also significantly advanced magnetic nanoparticle engineering, surface-functionalization strategies, and bio–nano interactions, shaping Switzerland’s expertise in translational nanomedicine. The Uni Fribourg team are experts in iron oxide magnetic nanomaterials, evidenced by their highly cited publications covering biomedical applications, in vivo/in vitro behavior and advanced in-silico modeling.3 4 5 6 Their recent paper7 reported on how ML models (built from detailed surface-chemistry descriptors) can be used to predict the toxicity of iron oxide magnetic nanoparticles. ML models typically address stability or structural features; no existing studies combine surface chemistry, toxicity and relaxivity into a single predictive framework.

Fleck will be the first project to integrate quantum-derived descriptors, molecular dynamics sampling, free-energy calculations and QML models into a single design loop for MRI contrast agents and their conjugates. For Gd- and Mn- drug conjugates and targeted theranostic systems, data and predictive tools are, at present, particularly scarce, presenting the opportune moment for this contribution to plug the research gap.

Hybrid quantum-AI platform

To the best of our knowledge, Amazon Braket Hybrid Jobs1 by Amazon Web Services (AWS) is the only commercial vendor to provide hybrid quantum-AI solutions in one workflow in a style most comparable to ours. Azure Quantum (Microsoft) offers “hybrid quantum computing”, which means something a little different and does not include AI or ML integration: “the classical code controls the execution of quantum operations based on mid-circuit measurements.”2 Neither of these are open source and neither is free. Google’s TensorFlow Quantum3 (developed with the TensorFlow team) allows developers to define quantum layers in classical neural networks to create a blended quantum-classical ML model, but does not allow one to orchestrate purely quantum circuits followed by purely classical ML models; moreover, in the last 5 years most ML experts have stopped using TensorFlow for their models.

For QPU or simulator use on AWS, one pays per shot, per task (or the corresponding reservation fees); for Microsoft, QPU time via partner providers must be paid separately, and those providers set their own pricing. Although there have been advances in quantum–AI workload scheduling, secure data management and scalable cloud architectures, full integration remains an open challenge due to hardware heterogeneity and limited standardisation—to use Azure Quantum, for instance, one is obliged to submit code in Microsoft’s proprietary programming language Q#.

There is currently no centralised quantum computing platform in Switzerland and there are no domestic quantum solutions with AI integration.

Fleck will be the first quantum computing platform in Switzerland to include full AI integration with quantum modalities and the first in the world to provide the code for the quantum-AI single job execution as open-source, the latter of which acknowledges the barrier posed to researchers worldwide who are otherwise limited by financial resources. Unlike Amazon Braket Hybrid Jobs, which promises that, “Once the algorithm has completed, you can then retrieve the results,”1 our stack incorporates MLFlow—an open-source code which tracks algorithms and experiments in a way that is not dissimilar to Git versioning. This allows the weights, losses and so forth to be recorded live and then stored in memory (S3) for later analysis, and will be of great significance to our ETH partner, who intend on running a long loop oscillating between a quantum computation and an ML algorithm; collecting time-stamped results will be indispensable for checking the code and catching any errors. AWS only offer monitoring through another proprietary paid service, SageMaker,4 which can track some (e.g., TensorFlow, PyTorch, MXNet, scikit-learn) but not all ML models. On the other hand, MLFlow is completely framework-agnostic, offers experiment tracking and model management, can integrate with any infrastructure or cloud and is free of charge.

Glossary

Terms

Binding affinity
The strength of the interaction between a drug and its target, influencing the drug’s distribution and potential to become trapped in tissues and thereby governing its overall effectiveness.
Complex
(noun) A chemical compound that has a central metal atom or ion.
Emulator
A classical computer running software that simulates the execution of a quantum circuit with full consideration given to the underlying physics of a specific quantum computing device. It thus includes a much deeper understanding of the lowest-level operations of such a device and is more demanding to run as a consequence.
FAIR
Data that meet the principles of “Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable”.
GAN
(Generative adversarial network) A set of two neural networks competing against each other: the generator network and the discriminator network. The role of the discriminator is to distinguish between real (input) data and fake (generated) data coming from the generator. The goal of the generator is to create fake data (which looks like real data) from an input of random noise. The training is an adversarial game until (ideally) a Nash equilibrium is reached: the discriminator has a 50% chance of distinguishing between real and generated data, indicating that the generator is faking data so well that it is undetectable.
Glioblastoma
A type of extremely invasive brain cancer—a malignant tumour of the glial tissue in the brain.
Latent space
Abstract mathematical space in which the input real-world data is encoded into a vector of numbers. The latent space is particularly useful for quantum networks as it helps to compress the complex input data into low-dimensional vectors, suitable for working with a limited number of qubits.
Phantom
A specially designed object (as opposed to human tissue) that is imaged in MRI to evaluate, analyse and calibrate the scanner.
qGAN
(Quantum GAN) A GAN where either the discriminator, the generator, or both, are replaced by a quantum neural network. Fleck uses a hybrid qGAN where the discriminator is a classical neural network and the generator is a quantum network.
QM/MM
(Quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics) A hybrid computational method that treats a small, chemically active region of a system with quantum mechanics, while the rest of the system is treated with computationally faster classical molecular mechanics.
Relaxometry
A measurement of tissue’s magnetic resonance relaxation properties, such as T2, T2* and T1.
Simulator
A classical computer running software that simulates the execution of a quantum circuit in a fashion that is agnostic to the underlying physics of the quantum computer. It can run simulations on state vectors or on density matrices and include errors.
Single-reference coupled cluster theory
A quantum chemistry method to describe how the electrons in a molecule behave by describing the system’s wave function as a mathematical correction (cluster operator) applied to a basic starting electron configuration.
Style-based approach
A technique inspired by classical AI where the input random noise of the quantum generator in qGANs is inserted in every gate of the quantum network and not only in the first layer of gates. This is an instance of data re-uploading where the input of the generator (the noise) is inserted everywhere in the network. It has been shown to extend the capabilities and accuracy of qGANs.
Theranostic
A combination of diagnosis and therapy to treat a disease in a single medical intervention.
VQE
(Variational quantum eigensolver) A hybrid algorithm in chemistry that uses both classical computers and quantum computers to find the ground state of a given physical system.

Quantum computation of biomolecular recognition

Biomolecular recognition describes the interaction of biomolecules in organisms—typically of a small molecule bound to a target protein. This interaction can be quantified by the free energy of ligand binding, which is computed by sampling molecular configuration space, for which standard classical approaches have been developed in the last few decades.1 2 The accuracy and hence reliability of these free-energy-of-binding predictions depends on the underlying potential energy surface, which is often provided by a tailored classical force field.3 A quantum description is, in principle, more reliable and applicable to all types of molecules without pre-parametrisation.

For quantum computations, the variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) has been the workhorse algorithm for current small-size quantum computers,4 but it has become clear that VQE-based quantum algorithms suffer from various severe drawbacks5 that will require one to turn to quantum phase estimation (QPE) based quantum algorithms on future fault-tolerant quantum hardware. QPE-based quantum energy measurements are typically considered for strongly correlated electronic structure problems, such as the notorious example of the mode of action (MoA) of nitrogenase.6 In all cases, any atomistic quantum simulations will always be restricted to a small number of atoms (compared to the huge number of atoms contained in a large protein). Accordingly, embedding approaches will thus always be required to exploit local quantum information within a biological macromolecule. Essentially this situation demands the unification of various traditional approaches in order to establish free energy calculations on a quantum computer, several attempts at which were published in 2025.7 8 9

Out of all existing approaches to compute biomolecular free energies of ligand binding, the FreeQuantum pipeline10 developed by consortium partner ETH, University of Copenhagen and MIT, is the most operationally mature. It is a unique accomplishment worldwide and the teams behind these ideas have recently received global recognition as finalists (team Q4proteins) in the XPRIZE competition.11

Fleck will be the first platform and infrastructure on which the FreeQuantum software will be available to execute by anyone in the world (developed by the Reiher group at ETH together with the University of Copenhagen and MIT). An implementation of the FreeQuantum pipeline within Fleck will allow users to obtain Hamiltonians which represent fully embedded fractions of biomolecules (on traditional HPC hardware) and which can then be loaded onto quantum computers through interfaces developed by QuantumBasel. Various technical challenges, interface structures and algorithmic developments will be necessary to establish such a tool. Their development will be carried out along the application put forward by the Uni Fribourg node. In particular, the most promising Gd-drug conjugates identified by Uni Basel will be modelled with quantum embedding methods available through FreeQuantum. The results of the latter branch will be integrated into the augmented database for ML refinement by Uni Fribourg.

Ion-trap quantum computers

Quantum computing hardware and emulators have become available through the cloud through a mix of proprietary access portals managed by individual vendors (e.g., IBM, AQT) and multi-platform providers such as Amazon AWS, Microsoft Azure, PinQ or OVHcloud. Other public bodies such as the LRZ (Leibniz Supercomputing Centre) in Munich provide access to quantum computers for the research community in Germany. These systems range in price, qubit count and data security, with machines ranging from 6 qubits to 120 across various platforms. In terms of trapped ion quantum computers, there is no cloud system based on a scalable architecture that is in line with the roadmaps of major vendors towards quantum utility.

ZuriQ is the only company in the world producing a 2D ion-trap quantum computer based on the Penning trap. The leading providers Quantinuum (USA) and IonQ (USA) have begun their own development of 2D systems, however these approaches inherently suffer from several scalability limitations resulting from the radio-frequency (rf) electric fields necessary to trap the ions. The rf fields restrict ion movement to 1-dimensional translations and leave large chip areas inaccessible, which significantly reduces qubit density per unit area. Moreover, any misalignment of ions from the null of the rf field—caused by uncompensated stray fields which are common and non-uniform across the chip—exposes the ions to the rf field, which degrades the fidelity of quantum operations. Looking forward, this imposes extreme demands on fabrication tolerances when integrating optical elements such as grating outcouplers, which must precisely focus laser light at the rf null to maintain the fidelity at a high level.

Finally, the rf signals (> 70 V amplitude) dissipate power into the substrate, causing substantial heating. This is particularly problematic when chips are fabricated using standard CMOS processes, since silicon exhibits a high loss tangent at cryogenic operating temperatures. By replacing the rf fields with a large and static magnetic field, the Penning trap method eliminates these constraints and is thus a much more natural platform for 2D ion arrays, with the potential to achieve an order of magnitude greater qubit density.1

ZuriQ is the only Swiss company producing commercial quantum computers and at present there is no Swiss quantum computer hosted on a cloud service. There is also no public cloud infrastructure that is accessible to public researchers along the lines of the LRZ model. The only single access point to locally installed quantum resources is through QuantumBasel, which has an IonQ trapped ion quantum computer with 36 qubits based on a non-scalable architecture installed on-premise. The last call for 1D (line) ion trap architectures is already chiming and their development has been abandoned by IonQ2 and Quantinuum3 in their roadmaps. At the time of writing, QuantumBasel is the only European company to sell pure access to both trapped-ion (IonQ, 36 qubits) and superconducting quantum computers (IBM Heron, 156 qubits), and the only company in the world to provide access to gate-based and annealing quantum computers (D-Wave) directly.

Other quantum hardware modalities are also being developed in Switzerland: the National Centre of Competence in Research Spin Qubits in Silicon (NCCR SPIN), headquartered at the University of Basel, is focussed on creating spin qubits using silicon and germanium to build a quantum computer. In 2024 the project demonstrated a single two-qubit gate,4 but the technology is still some way away from leaving the university environment.

Fleck will contribute to the development of the first commercial sovereign Swiss quantum computer, and the world’s first 2D Penning trap ion quantum computer.

Ultra-low field magnetic resonance imaging

Globally, ultra-low-field (ULF) MRI is a rapidly advancing research domain driven by breakthroughs in cryogen-free magnetics, advanced noise cancellation and quantum-inspired detection technologies. No standardized ULF relaxometry methodology exists, and nanoparticle contrast agents are still optimized exclusively at clinical field strengths (1.5–7 T), where relaxation mechanisms differ fundamentally from ULF. Phantom designs, when available, lack reproducibility and do not reflect nanoparticle binding behavior. Thus, despite significant progress, there is no validated pipeline connecting phantom characterization, ULF relaxometry sequences, and predictive quantum modeling of nanoparticle relaxation.

Multiwave Technologies is the only Swiss company producing ULF MRI scanners. Switzerland leads biomedical imaging innovation, with important contributions and advances in MRI and nanomedicine coming from EPFL and ETH Zürich. Targeted complexes, precision relaxometry and model-based reconstruction are strong national competencies, but despite this, ULF relaxometry with protein-specific metal-based contrast agents remains unaddressed, with no validated phantom standards. Establishing Swiss capability here presents a timely leadership opportunity in a strategically emerging field.
